How AI learned to look it up before it answers

The 2020 paper behind every AI that shows its sources, explained with pictures, a few toys, and as few words as possible.

22 MAY 2020

34th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2020)

Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks

Patrick Lewis · Ethan Perez · Aleksandra Piktus · Fabio Petroni · Vladimir Karpukhin · Naman Goyal · Heinrich Küttler · Mike Lewis · Wen-tau Yih · Tim Rocktäschel · Sebastian Riedel · Douwe Kiela

Facebook AI Research · University College London · New York University

1

Glue on pizza?

In 2024 an AI search answer told people to add glue to pizza sauce. It didn’t make that up. It looked it up, in an 11-year-old joke.

Looking things up before answering is called RAG. It comes from this paper.

Outside the paperGoogle, May 30, 2024: “…AI Overviews that featured sarcastic or troll-y content from discussion forums.”

2

From memory, it makes things up.

An AI that answers only from memory can invent a fact and say it like a fact. The paper shows one.

In the paperparametric memory (facts blended into the model) and non-parametric memory (a library it can read). Table 3 marks BART’s answer as wrong; the paper’s word for it is hallucination.

3

Look it up first: R, A, G.

RAG gives the AI the habit you already have: check, then answer. Three steps.

In the papera retriever (a model called DPR) and a generator (BART-large, 400 million parameters)

4

A library of 21 million cards.

They cut all of Wikipedia into cards of 100 words each. That makes 21 million cards.

In the paperWikipedia as of December 2018, split into 100-word passages: 21 million of them, searched with a fast index (MIPS, built with FAISS).

5

How it picks: multiply and add.

Your question gets a few numbers: how much it is about pizza, about cheese, about cars. Every card has the same three. Multiply the matching numbers, add them up, and fetch the biggest totals.

In the paperthe retriever, DPR: two BERT readers turn every question and every card into 768 numbers (section 2.2).

6

Five drafts, one answer.

It doesn’t bet on one card. Each of the five cards helps write a draft, and each draft counts as much as its card is trusted. Then it adds them up.

In the paperRAG-Sequence: each card writes a whole answer, and the answers are weighed by trust and added up (section 2.1).

7

Or switch cards on every word.

RAG has a second version that can lean on a different card for every word it writes. Asked for a quiz clue about “Hemingway”, it leaned on one card per book.

In the paperRAG-Token, Figure 2. After the first word of each title, the lean fades: the writer finishes the title from memory.

8

No answer key.

Nobody told it which card was right. It practised on questions with known answers, and when an answer came out right, the card it used scored higher next time.

In the papertrained end to end, with no labels for which card helps. Only the question reader (the query encoder) and the writer (BART) learn; the library and its index stay fixed (section 2.4).

9

Better answers, from a smaller model.

On real questions people typed into Google, it set a new record, beat a model 17 times its size, and judges found its answers more factual.

In the paperTable 1 (Natural Questions, exact match) and Table 4 (452 pairs judged by people). It even got 11.8% right when none of its cards held the answer.

10

Swap the library, not the AI.

To teach it new facts, you don’t retrain it. You swap the library. The paper asked about 82 world leaders who changed between 2016 and 2018.

In the paperindex hot-swapping: 70% and 68% right when the library matches the year, 12% and 4% when it doesn’t.

11

Where it went.

Under three years later, the new Bing answered with sources. Now, when an AI says “searching” and shows little numbered links, that’s RAG.

Outside the paperMicrosoft, Feb 7, 2023: “The new Bing also cites all its sources…”

The whole idea, in four steps

  1. Turn the question into numbers, and fetch the 5 cards that match best.
  2. Clip each card to the question.
  3. Write a draft from each card, and count each by how much its card is trusted.
  4. Keep the library fresh and clean. Garbage in, glue out.
